
How do I design blobby architectural forms like those of Greg Lynn and Zaha Hadid? Not all the amazing detail they go into but just the macro forms, which are characterized by being both organic and structurally sound.


The key insight is that "organic" and "structurally sound" aren't in tension for these forms. Double-curved surfaces are stiff because of their curvature, the way an eggshell is. So the design process has two parts: generating a blobby form, then letting structural logic push the form toward its final shape.
Generating the form

Metaballs / implicit surfaces (the Greg Lynn approach). Lynn's "blobs" were literally metaballs: points or primitives that each radiate a field, with a surface drawn where the combined field hits a threshold. When two blobs get close, they fuse smoothly instead of intersecting. This gives you organic merging almost for free. You can do this in Blender (Add → Metaball), Houdini, or Grasshopper (Dendro or Cocoon plugins). Place a few spheres and ellipsoids, adjust their strengths, and watch them melt together. Lynn also animated these fields over time (forces, attractors, gravity) and froze a frame as the building, which is a good way to get forms that feel "caught in motion."

Subdivision modeling (closer to Hadid's office practice). Build a coarse low-poly cage (a box, a stretched torus, a few extruded arms) and let subdivision smooth it into a continuous surface. This gives you much more control than metaballs over where edges soften and where they sharpen. Rhino 7+ SubD, Blender's Subdivision Surface modifier, and Maya all work well. Hadid's forms often read as a single continuous skin that folds from ground to wall to roof, and SubD is excellent at that.

Lofting through sections. Draw a series of plan or section curves that morph gradually (a circle that stretches into a slot, then splits) and loft a surface through them. This is a quick way to get the long, flowing, directional quality of Hadid projects like the Heydar Aliyev Center.
Making it structurally sound

This is where form-finding comes in, a tradition going back to Gaudí's hanging chain models, Frei Otto, and Heinz Isler's hanging-fabric shells. A chain hanging under its own weight takes a shape in pure tension, and flipped upside down that shape is in pure compression, which is ideal for an arch or shell. You can do this digitally:

Kangaroo (a Grasshopper physics plugin) lets you take your rough mesh, pin the support points, apply gravity, and let it relax. The result keeps the character of your design but gets pulled toward a funicular shape. RhinoVAULT (from ETH's Block Research Group) does the same for compression-only shells, starting from a plan drawing. Karamba3D is a structural analysis plugin that shows stress and deflection directly on your form, so you can see where it's weak.

A few macro-scale rules of thumb, even without simulation:

    Keep curvature everywhere. Flat or nearly flat regions are where shells get weak and need thickness or beams. Synclastic curvature (dome-like, curving the same way in both directions) and anticlastic curvature (saddle-like, curving opposite ways) are both stiff.
    Let the form flow into its supports. Hadid's buildings often seem to "pour" into the ground. Structurally this means loads travel smoothly down the surface instead of concentrating at sharp points.
    Avoid long cantilevers with low curvature unless you're willing to hide a lot of steel inside.
    Edges need stiffening. A free shell edge is floppy, so either curl it (Isler's shells curve their edges up), thicken it, or give it a ring beam.
    Check continuity. Surfaces with smooth curvature continuity (G2) both look more organic and avoid stress concentrations at kinks.

A practical workflow

Sketch a rough massing as a SubD cage or a few metaballs. Convert to a mesh, bring it into Grasshopper, pin the supports, and relax it in Kangaroo with gravity plus some "stay close to original" goal, so it doesn't drift too far from your intent. Then run it through Karamba to spot weak areas, adjust, and repeat. Rhino + Grasshopper is the most complete ecosystem for this. Blender with its geometry nodes and physics can get you surprisingly far if you want something free.

For reading, Lynn's Animate Form explains his thinking directly, and Philippe Block's work on RhinoVAULT and thrust network analysis is the clearest modern treatment of how to make free-form shells stand up.

It can render them directly, but you almost certainly shouldn't. Ship polygons.

Why direct rendering is a bad fit. Metaballs can be drawn directly on the GPU by raymarching a signed distance field in a shader. VRChat worlds can use custom shaders on Quest, so it's technically possible. But raymarching costs a lot per pixel, and the Quest 3 is a mobile GPU rendering two high-resolution eyes at 72–90 fps. Fill rate is exactly where it's weakest, so a raymarched building that fills your view would tank frame rates. There are other problems too. Raymarched surfaces don't get physics colliders, so you'd need a mesh anyway to walk on. They can't take baked lighting. And writing correct depth from a raymarching shader disables some of the GPU's early rejection optimizations. Raymarching is fine for a small decorative object, but not for the architecture itself.

Discretize, but do the modeling in the field domain. Your slicing plan fits the SDF world well, so do the cuts before meshing rather than as polygon booleans afterward. In distance-field terms:

    Blending blobs is a smooth union (smooth-min) of the fields.
    Cutting with a plane is an intersection or subtraction with the plane's distance field. You can make it a smooth subtraction so the cut edges get a soft fillet instead of a knife-sharp line, which does a lot for the "solid and calm" feeling.
    Hollowing into a shell is abs(d) - thickness. This matters: when you slice an occupiable space open, you want to reveal a wall with believable thickness, not a paper-thin surface. Thick, rounded edges at openings are what make these forms feel safe to stand in.

Then run marching cubes once to get a mesh. Good tools for this include Blender Geometry Nodes (Points to Volume → volume operations → Volume to Mesh gets you most of the way; Blender's native metaball objects can also be converted to mesh), MagicaCSG (a free, very pleasant SDF modeler that exports meshes), and libfive if you like code.

Cleanup for Quest. Marching cubes output is dense and messy, so remesh and decimate it. Blender's Decimate or QuadriFlow, or Instant Meshes, work well. Smooth blobby surfaces tolerate decimation well in flat-ish regions and need density only where curvature is tight. Keep the whole visible scene to a modest triangle budget, since Quest worlds perform best in the low hundreds of thousands of triangles at most. Also build separate, much simpler collision meshes, because walking on a dense organic mesh is costly and can feel bumpy.

The lighting is what sells it. Plain white organic forms live or die by lighting. Under flat real-time lighting they look like undifferentiated white blobs with no sense of scale or solidity. Bake lightmaps with strong, soft ambient occlusion and bounce light. Bakery is the go-to lightmapper in the VRChat community, and Unity's built-in one works too. The gentle gradients where a wall curves into a floor are what make the space read as massive and grounded. Baked lighting is also nearly free at runtime on Quest, so it serves both your goals at once. Plan for clean, non-overlapping lightmap UVs on the final mesh, which is another reason to remesh rather than ship raw marching-cubes output.
